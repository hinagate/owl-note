Explaining this to myself properly instead of trusting a calculator I don't
understand. The compound interest formula:

$$A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}$$

where $P$ is the principal, $r$ the annual rate (as a decimal), $n$ the number of
times it compounds per year, and $t$ the number of years.

Worked example — money I actually parked in the savings account:
- $P = 5000$
- $r = 0.05$ (5% a year)
- $n = 12$ (compounds monthly)
- $t = 10$ years

$$A = 5000\left(1 + \frac{0.05}{12}\right)^{120} \approx 8235$$

So five grand becomes about **\$8,235** after ten years, i.e. roughly \$3,235 of
interest. The thing that surprised me: monthly compounding ($n = 12$) versus
annual only adds about \$40 over the decade at this rate. The rate and the *time*
do the heavy lifting, not the compounding frequency.
